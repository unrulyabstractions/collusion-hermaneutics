Agent swarms on large, often closed models can collude, and their monitor must be cheap. We study a hermeneutic probe: a linear readout of a small open-weight reader model's internals while it reads the transcript of a larger author model. Five authors ran nine single-agent tasks with a planted exploit, with and without a prompt naming it. We teacher-force a stratified subsample of 600 transcripts through Qwen3.8-27B and probe its residual stream at the think tokens. Without the prompt, one probe pooled over authors separates cheating from honest work with AUROC 0.967 on held-out runs. It keeps 0.944 after author, task and prompt condition are linearly erased, reaches 0.80 to 0.97 on authors it never saw, and beats three cheap LLM judges on cut transcripts (0.62 to 0.80). A bag-of-words classifier on the full transcript matches or exceeds it (0.979), because here the exploit text enters the context through tool output. Matched runs where lexical cues cancel are the decisive next test. With three agents, one planted board message lifts coordination off its floor, and a mild nudge yields more teams that assemble the exploit and pass the hidden tests than an explicit one (directional: five seeds per arm, no significance test). In the sources used here, only cheating has direct labels at scale for our authors. We therefore propose the reader's internals as a cheap monitor for closed authors and name multi-agent data as the binding constraint.
